Popular CMS Platforms and Their Features

▶

A Content Management System (CMS) is the software backbone behind virtually every type of modern website, from personal blogs and small business storefronts to sprawling enterprise portals and global e-commerce operations. Rather than requiring site owners to hand-code every page in HTML, a CMS provides a structured environment where content can be created, organized, edited, and published without deep programming knowledge. However, not all CMS platforms are built the same way, and choosing the wrong one for a project can lead to wasted development time, unnecessary costs, and painful migrations down the road. Understanding the landscape of available platforms — their architectures, ecosystems, ideal use cases, and real limitations — is one of the most practical skills a web professional or digital project manager can develop. This topic offers an in-depth examination of the most widely used CMS platforms today, from the ubiquitous WordPress to enterprise-grade Drupal, specialized e-commerce systems like Shopify, beginner-friendly hosted builders like Wix and Squarespace, and the increasingly popular headless CMS model.

WordPress: The World's Most Popular CMS

WordPress powers roughly 43% of all websites on the internet as of recent surveys — a market share so dominant that it dwarfs every other CMS combined. Originally launched in 2003 as a blogging platform, WordPress has evolved into a full-featured CMS capable of running complex publishing networks, membership communities, online stores, and corporate websites. Its success rests on three foundational pillars: an accessible user interface, an unmatched plugin ecosystem, and a massive global community of developers, designers, and educators.

The WordPress dashboard is the administrative hub where everything happens. After logging in, users are greeted by a clean sidebar navigation linking to posts, pages, media, comments, appearance settings, plugins, and users. The interface is deliberately approachable — a non-technical site owner can publish an article, upload images, or change a site's color scheme without ever touching a line of code. Since WordPress 5.0, content creation has centered on the Gutenberg block editor, a visual, block-based system where each paragraph, image, video, button, or custom component is an independent block that can be dragged, reordered, and configured individually. Gutenberg represents a significant shift away from the older Classic Editor (still available as a plugin), giving editors much finer control over page layout without requiring a separate page-builder plugin.

WordPress themes control the visual design and overall layout of a site. The official Theme Directory alone hosts thousands of free themes, while premium marketplaces like ThemeForest offer thousands more. Many modern themes are built on frameworks like Genesis or use page builders like Elementor or Divi to offer drag-and-drop customization far beyond what the core block editor provides. Switching themes can dramatically change a site's appearance in minutes, though complex theme switches often require reconfiguration of custom layouts and widgets.

The plugin ecosystem is arguably WordPress's most powerful differentiator. With over 60,000 free plugins available in the official directory — and countless premium options sold independently — virtually any feature can be bolted onto a WordPress site:

  • SEO: Plugins like Yoast SEO and Rank Math provide on-page optimization analysis, XML sitemap generation, schema markup, and social media metadata management.
  • Contact and lead generation: WPForms, Gravity Forms, and Contact Form 7 allow editors to build multi-step forms, surveys, and quizzes without custom code.
  • Membership and LMS: MemberPress, LearnDash, and LifterLMS turn WordPress into a fully featured course platform or gated membership community.
  • E-commerce: WooCommerce is the most significant plugin in the entire WordPress ecosystem. It transforms any WordPress installation into a complete online store, supporting physical products, digital downloads, subscriptions, variable products, tax calculation, and hundreds of payment gateways. WooCommerce itself has its own extension library with over 800 official add-ons.
  • Performance and caching: WP Rocket, W3 Total Cache, and LiteSpeed Cache reduce server load and improve page speed through caching, lazy loading, and asset minification.
  • Security: Wordfence and Sucuri provide malware scanning, firewall protection, brute-force login prevention, and activity logging.

WordPress is best suited for blogs, news publications, portfolios, small-to-medium business sites, and content-heavy projects where the primary audience is editors and marketers rather than developers. Its low barrier to entry and abundance of learning resources make it the default starting point for most web projects.

However, WordPress has real limitations that become apparent at scale or in specialized contexts. Because of its enormous popularity, WordPress installations are a constant target for automated attacks exploiting vulnerable plugins, outdated themes, or weak credentials. Keeping a WordPress site secure demands disciplined update management. On the performance side, sites running large numbers of plugins can suffer from bloated database queries and slow load times; achieving enterprise-level performance typically requires a managed hosting environment (such as WP Engine or Kinsta), a CDN, aggressive caching, and ongoing technical maintenance. Additionally, WordPress's flexible architecture means that poorly coded plugins can conflict with one another or introduce instability, placing a burden on site administrators to vet and maintain their plugin stack carefully.

Joomla: Flexible Mid-Tier CMS

Joomla is the second most widely used open-source CMS in terms of active installations, sitting between the simplicity of WordPress and the complexity of Drupal. It was forked from the Mambo CMS in 2005 and has developed a loyal following in sectors where structured access control and native multilingual support are priorities. Joomla is not as beginner-friendly as WordPress, but it rewards users who invest time in learning it with a notably more structured and flexible content architecture out of the box.

One of Joomla's most distinctive built-in features is its Access Control Level (ACL) system. Where WordPress treats user roles as relatively flat categories (Administrator, Editor, Author, Contributor, Subscriber), Joomla allows administrators to define custom user groups, assign specific viewing and editing permissions at the level of individual articles, menu items, or entire sections of the site, and control exactly who can publish, delete, or manage content. This granular control is invaluable for organizations where different departments or teams need to manage their own content independently while being prevented from modifying content outside their scope — a common requirement in government agencies, universities, and large corporate intranets.

Joomla also provides native multilingual support built directly into the core system. Setting up a multilingual Joomla site does not require a third-party plugin; the language manager, content associations, and language-specific menus are all built in. This is a meaningful advantage over WordPress, where multilingual functionality typically depends on plugins like WPML (a premium paid plugin) or Polylang, adding cost and complexity. For organizations publishing content in multiple languages — particularly international NGOs, government bodies, or multinational companies — Joomla's built-in multilingual architecture can simplify site management considerably.

Joomla extends its functionality through an extension directory that organizes add-ons into three distinct types:

  • Components: Major functional extensions that have their own administrative backend, such as e-commerce systems (VirtueMart), forums (Kunena), or event managers. A component is the heaviest type of Joomla extension and typically adds an entirely new section to the admin panel.
  • Modules: Smaller display units positioned in layout positions around the page, such as a login form, a search box, or a news ticker. Modules are lighter than components and control specific areas of the page template.
  • Plugins: Event-driven extensions that execute in response to specific triggers, such as a plugin that automatically formats certain text, integrates a payment gateway, or authenticates users against an external directory.

Joomla's typical use cases include community portals, membership websites, association management systems, government websites, and corporate intranets — particularly where access control, structured content, and multilingual delivery are core requirements rather than optional add-ons.

The platform's main limitations are a steeper initial learning curve than WordPress and a smaller developer community, which translates to fewer themes, fewer extensions, and less freely available documentation. Site builders accustomed to WordPress's intuitive admin experience may find Joomla's interface more complex and less immediately familiar.

Drupal: Enterprise-Grade Content Management

Drupal occupies the high end of the open-source CMS spectrum. It is the platform of choice for governments, universities, large nonprofits, and enterprises that need to model complex content relationships, enforce rigorous security policies, and handle high-traffic loads reliably. Notable Drupal-powered sites include NASA, the Government of Australia, and many major universities. It is not a platform most beginners should approach without developer support, but for projects that genuinely require its power, it has no peer among open-source alternatives.

Drupal's most technically significant feature is its content modeling system. Unlike WordPress, where a "post" or "page" is largely a fixed container with a title, body, and metadata, Drupal allows administrators to define entirely custom content types — each with its own unique set of fields, field types (text, number, date, entity reference, file, image, geolocation, and more), and display configurations. These content types can reference one another, creating relational structures analogous to a proper relational database. For example, a university website might have a "Faculty Member" content type with fields for department, biography, photo, and publications, and a "Research Project" content type that references multiple faculty members and associated publications. This structured modeling is extremely difficult to replicate cleanly in WordPress without heavy customization, and Joomla does not approach Drupal's depth in this area.

Drupal's security track record is exceptional by CMS standards. A dedicated Drupal Security Team monitors vulnerabilities, coordinates responsible disclosure, and publishes security advisories on a regular schedule. Drupal's codebase is subject to rigorous peer review, and its permission system is highly granular, allowing fine-tuned control over what each user role can view, create, edit, or delete. Government agencies with strict compliance requirements (including those subject to frameworks like FedRAMP in the United States) frequently choose Drupal precisely because of this security posture.

The Views module — now bundled in Drupal core since Drupal 8 — is one of the most powerful tools the CMS offers. Views allows administrators to build custom displays of content: filtered lists, sortable tables, image galleries, calendars, slideshows, and data grids — all through a visual interface without writing custom PHP queries. A site manager can create a view that displays all research papers from the last five years in a given department, sorted by publication date, filtered by author, and formatted as either a table or a teaser list — all without touching code. This is enormously valuable for content-rich websites where editorial teams need to surface specific subsets of content in multiple different ways.

Drupal's limitations are real and should not be minimized. The learning curve is steep — even experienced web developers typically require dedicated Drupal training before becoming productive. The theming system, site configuration workflows, and the conceptual shift from "pages with content" to "structured entities with fields" all require significant adjustment. Development and maintenance costs are substantially higher than for WordPress or Joomla, making Drupal a poor choice for small businesses or simple informational websites where that investment cannot be justified by the complexity of the content or the scale of the operation.

Shopify and E-Commerce-Focused Platforms

Shopify is a fully hosted, software-as-a-service (SaaS) e-commerce platform designed from the ground up for selling products online. Unlike WordPress with WooCommerce (which builds e-commerce on top of a general-purpose CMS), Shopify treats commerce as the primary function, with everything else — blog posts, pages, navigation — being secondary. This singular focus gives Shopify a significant advantage in ease of setup and out-of-the-box commerce capabilities.

From day one, a Shopify merchant has access to integrated payment processing via Shopify Payments (powered by Stripe), inventory tracking across multiple warehouse locations, shipping rate calculation, label printing integrations (UPS, FedEx, USPS, DHL), tax calculation, order management, and a real-time analytics dashboard showing revenue, conversion rates, average order value, and customer behavior. Setting up a basic store and accepting payments can be accomplished in a single afternoon without any developer involvement.

Shopify's App Store extends the platform's capabilities significantly, with thousands of apps covering:

  • Email marketing automation (Klaviyo, Omnisend)
  • Loyalty and rewards programs (Smile.io, LoyaltyLion)
  • Dropshipping fulfillment (DSers, Spocket)
  • Subscriptions and recurring billing (Recharge)
  • Multichannel selling across Amazon, eBay, TikTok Shop, Instagram, and Facebook Shops
  • Advanced product reviews and user-generated content tools

Shopify scales impressively. Its infrastructure is managed entirely by Shopify, meaning merchants do not need to worry about server capacity, security patches, or uptime during high-traffic events like Black Friday. Shopify Plus, the enterprise tier, powers some of the world's largest direct-to-consumer brands and can handle enormous transaction volumes without merchant-side configuration.

The limitations of Shopify are primarily financial and structural. Monthly subscription fees range from basic plans to Shopify Plus contracts that can cost thousands of dollars per month. When merchants use third-party payment processors instead of Shopify Payments, Shopify charges an additional transaction fee on every sale — a meaningful cost at scale. Template customization uses Shopify's proprietary Liquid templating language, which developers must learn specifically for Shopify work. And while the platform is highly capable within its e-commerce domain, it is not designed to support highly custom content architectures, complex editorial workflows, or non-commerce use cases — merchants needing those features will need to integrate external tools or accept workarounds.

Other notable e-commerce-focused CMS platforms include BigCommerce (similar to Shopify in its hosted model but with fewer transaction fees and more built-in features on lower-tier plans), Magento/Adobe Commerce (an open-source platform for enterprise retailers who need maximum customization and are willing to manage their own infrastructure), and PrestaShop (a popular open-source option in European markets).

Wix and Squarespace: Hosted Website Builders

Wix and Squarespace represent a category of CMS platform distinct from WordPress, Joomla, and Drupal: fully hosted, all-in-one website builders marketed primarily to individuals and small businesses who want a professional-looking website without any technical setup. Both platforms have grown substantially in sophistication, now offering e-commerce, blogging, booking systems, and basic membership features alongside their core site-building tools.

Wix differentiates itself through maximum visual freedom. Its drag-and-drop editor allows users to place any element — text, image, button, video, map, form — anywhere on the canvas with pixel-level precision, unconstrained by a grid or fixed column system. For users who want complete control over the visual layout without learning CSS, this is genuinely liberating. Wix also introduced Wix ADI (Artificial Design Intelligence), an AI-driven onboarding flow that asks users a series of questions about their business and goals, then generates a complete, personalized website design automatically — arguably the fastest path from zero to a published website available anywhere. Wix's app market adds additional functionality including booking systems (Wix Bookings), event management (Wix Events), and restaurant ordering tools (Wix Restaurants).

Squarespace takes a different design philosophy. Rather than unconstrained freedom, Squarespace offers a curated selection of award-winning templates built on a sophisticated grid system, ensuring that sites look polished and professional even when edited by non-designers. Squarespace templates are particularly celebrated for their typography, whitespace, and visual elegance — qualities that make the platform especially popular among photographers, graphic designers, architects, restaurants, and creative professionals for whom visual presentation is paramount. Squarespace also includes strong e-commerce capabilities (including product management, abandoned cart recovery, and subscription products) and a robust blogging engine.

Both platforms include the following bundled services in their subscription plans:

  • Web hosting with automatic scaling
  • Free SSL certificates (HTTPS) on all plans
  • Automatic software updates — users never need to patch the platform manually
  • Integrated domain registration (or domain connection)
  • Customer support via chat and email

The limitations of these platforms become apparent for users with more advanced needs. Neither Wix nor Squarespace supports custom server-side code, external databases, or deeply custom application logic in the way that a self-hosted WordPress or Drupal installation does. Developers cannot install arbitrary plugins or extensions outside the official app marketplaces. The freedom to migrate content away from these platforms is restricted — exporting all content in a portable, reusable format is difficult or impossible in some cases, creating a form of platform lock-in that should be carefully considered before committing to either. For small businesses, freelancers, and individuals whose needs fit within the platforms' capabilities, these limitations are rarely a practical problem. For growing organizations whose requirements may evolve significantly, they can become significant constraints.

Headless CMS Platforms: Decoupled Content Delivery

The headless CMS model represents a fundamental architectural departure from traditional CMS platforms. In a traditional (or "coupled") CMS like WordPress or Joomla, the same system manages both the content repository (where content is stored, structured, and edited) and the presentation layer (the themes, templates, and HTML that users see in their browsers). These two layers are tightly coupled — the CMS decides how content is displayed. In a headless CMS, the content repository is separated entirely from any presentation layer. The CMS stores and organizes structured content and exposes it via an API (typically a RESTful API or a GraphQL endpoint). Developers then build whatever front-end they choose — a React application, a Next.js site, a Vue.js app, a native mobile application, a smart TV interface, a voice assistant — consuming the API to retrieve content and rendering it however they see fit.

This architecture offers several compelling advantages:

  • Omnichannel content delivery: The same content stored once in the CMS can be delivered to a website, a mobile app, a digital kiosk, an IoT device, or any other digital touchpoint — all simultaneously, from a single source of truth.
  • Front-end technology freedom: Development teams are not constrained by the CMS's templating system. They can build with the latest JavaScript frameworks, use static site generators like Gatsby or Astro, or leverage edge rendering for maximum performance.
  • Performance: Headless sites are often built as static sites or server-side rendered applications deployed on CDN infrastructure, resulting in extremely fast load times that would be difficult to achieve with a traditional database-driven CMS.
  • Scalability: Because the front-end is decoupled from the backend, each can be scaled independently based on demand.

Popular headless CMS platforms include Contentful, Sanity, Strapi (open-source), Prismic, Directus, and Storyblok. Some traditional CMS platforms — including WordPress — can also operate in a headless mode, using the WordPress REST API or WPGraphQL to serve content to a decoupled front-end while retaining the familiar WordPress editorial interface.

The trade-offs of the headless approach are significant for teams without strong developer resources. There are no pre-built themes, no visual page builders, and no drag-and-drop design tools — every aspect of the user-facing experience must be designed and coded from scratch. Editorial teams lose the live preview and WYSIWYG editing experience that traditional CMS platforms offer. The infrastructure setup is more complex, typically involving a CMS subscription, a separate hosting service for the front-end (Vercel, Netlify, AWS), and a CI/CD pipeline for deploying updates. For organizations with capable development teams building custom digital experiences across multiple channels, these trade-offs are entirely worthwhile. For a small business that needs a website up in a week, they are prohibitive.

Choosing the Right CMS: Strengths, Limitations, and Use Cases

Selecting a CMS is not simply a technical decision — it is a business decision with long-term consequences for operational costs, team workflows, content flexibility, and vendor relationships. There is no single "best" CMS; every platform represents a set of trade-offs, and the right choice depends entirely on the specific requirements, resources, and long-term trajectory of the project.

When evaluating platforms, one of the most important — and frequently overlooked — considerations is total cost of ownership (TCO). The nominal licensing cost of an open-source CMS like WordPress or Drupal is zero, but the actual cost of a professionally managed WordPress site includes hosting fees, premium theme licenses, premium plugin subscriptions, security monitoring services, developer time for maintenance and customization, and periodic major version upgrades. A Shopify store has transparent monthly fees but adds up in transaction fees, app subscriptions, and theme costs. A headless CMS stack may involve a paid CMS tier, a hosting platform subscription, and significant ongoing developer time. Projecting these costs over a two-to-five-year horizon — rather than evaluating only the initial setup cost — gives a far more accurate picture of what each platform truly costs the organization.

Beyond cost, decision-makers should evaluate:

  • Team capability: Who will manage the site day-to-day? A platform that requires developer involvement for routine content updates creates a bottleneck. A platform with a visual editor empowers editorial staff but may constrain developers.
  • Content complexity: Simple pages and blog posts can live comfortably in any CMS. Complex, interrelated content types with custom fields, taxonomies, and structured data relationships demand Drupal-level content modeling or a well-structured headless CMS.
  • Growth trajectory: A platform that handles today's needs perfectly but cannot scale to tomorrow's traffic volume, content volume, or feature requirements will eventually require a costly migration.
  • Data portability: Platforms that make it difficult to export content in standard formats create lock-in risk. Organizations should understand what it would take to migrate away from their chosen platform before committing to it.
  • Regulatory and compliance requirements: Healthcare, government, and financial services organizations may have specific accessibility (WCAG), data residency, or security compliance requirements that favor certain platforms over others.

In practice, WordPress remains the default recommendation for the broadest range of projects — its combination of ease of use, ecosystem depth, and community support is unmatched at its price point. Drupal and Joomla are the right answer when organizational requirements genuinely demand their specific strengths in content modeling or access control. Shopify is the clear winner for straightforward retail e-commerce where time-to-market and operational simplicity matter more than customization depth. Wix and Squarespace serve individuals and small businesses who want professional results with minimal technical involvement and whose requirements are unlikely to outgrow the platforms' capabilities. And headless CMS platforms are increasingly the architecture of choice for sophisticated digital teams building custom, high-performance, multi-channel experiences — a space that will only grow as the complexity of digital content distribution continues to increase.

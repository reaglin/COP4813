Introduction

A content management system (CMS) is software that separates the content of a website from its presentation and code, allowing people who are not developers to create, edit, organize, and publish pages through an administrative interface. Behind that interface sit a few predictable parts: a database or content store, an editing and authoring environment, a templating or theming layer that controls appearance, and an extension mechanism (plugins, modules, apps) that adds functionality. Understanding these pieces is what lets you reason about any platform you encounter later, whether it is WordPress, Joomla, Drupal, Shopify, or a headless service.

This topic matters in COP4813 because the rest of the module asks you to make judgments, not just to click through dashboards. Before you can compare platforms or defend a recommendation of "CMS" versus "custom build" for a client, you need a clear mental model of how a CMS fits into the web ecosystem: where content lives, who touches it, what the server does at request time, and what parts a developer still owns. You will also meet the distinction between traditional (coupled) systems, where the CMS renders the pages it stores, and headless systems, which deliver content over an API to a front end you build separately.

The three videos below move from the general idea to two specific modern variations. While watching, keep a running list of the components each presenter mentions, note the vocabulary they use for content types and templates, and ask yourself what kind of project each approach would suit — a small brochure site, a large editorial operation, or an application front end that needs content from multiple channels.
